Laminate and vinyl plank flooring are sold by the box, and every box covers a fixed area. Order too little and you may not find the same batch again; order too much and you are stuck with boxes you cannot always return. Getting the number right takes three steps: the floor area, a waste allowance and the coverage printed on the box.

## Step 1: Measure the floor area

For a rectangular room, measure the length and width at floor level, wall to wall, and multiply them.

**Area = length × width**

A room that is 14 ft by 12 ft has **168 square feet** of floor. In metric, a room of 4 m by 3 m has 12 m².

If the room is not a rectangle, split it into rectangles, work out each one and add them up. Our guide to [measuring square footage](/blog/how-to-measure-square-footage/) shows how to handle L-shaped rooms, and the [square footage calculator](/calculators/square-footage-calculator/) adds several areas for you. Remember closets and alcoves that will get the same floor.

## Step 2: Add a waste allowance

You will always cut planks at the end of each row, around door frames and along uneven walls. Some offcuts can start the next row, but not all of them. That is what the waste allowance is for.

| Layout | Typical allowance |
| --- | --- |
| Straight rows in a simple rectangular room | about 10% |
| Rooms with many corners, columns or doorways | 10–15% |
| Diagonal layout | around 15% |
| Herringbone or chevron | more; check the manufacturer's advice |

For the 14 × 12 ft room with straight rows: 168 × 1.10 = **184.8 square feet**.

## Step 3: Divide by the coverage per box

Every box states how much floor it covers, usually in both square feet and square meters. It varies with plank size and thickness, so always read your actual box rather than assuming.

If a box covers 20 sq ft:

184.8 ÷ 20 = 9.24, which rounds **up to 10 boxes** (200 sq ft of flooring).

Always round up. You cannot buy 0.24 of a box, and running short in the last row is the most common flooring mistake.

## What about underlayment?

Most floating floors need an underlayment unless one is already attached to the planks. It is sold by area too, so plan for about the floor area itself (here 168 sq ft), plus a little for overlaps if the product requires them. On concrete subfloors a vapor barrier is often needed as well. Follow the flooring manufacturer's instructions, because using the wrong underlayment can void the warranty.

## Baseboards and trim

New flooring usually means new baseboards (skirting), and the small accessories are what people forget. For a rectangular room:

- **Baseboard length** is the room's perimeter minus the doorways, plus about 10% for cuts. The 14 × 12 ft room has a 52 ft perimeter; with one 3 ft doorway that is 49 ft, or about 54 ft with waste. In 8 ft pieces that is **7 pieces**.
- **Inside corners**: a rectangular room has four. Add one for each alcove corner.
- **Outside corners** where a wall sticks out, such as a chimney breast or the end of a partition.
- **End caps** where the baseboard stops at a door frame, usually two per doorway, if your system uses them.
- **Connectors** where two pieces meet along a long wall.
- **Transition strips** (thresholds) at each doorway where the new floor meets a different floor.

Systems differ: some profiles are mitred at the corners and need no corner pieces, others clip into plastic corners and caps. Check what your baseboard range offers before you buy.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring from the baseboard instead of the wall.** Measure wall to wall; the expansion gap under the baseboard is small and the waste allowance covers it.
- **Forgetting the expansion gap.** Floating floors need a gap around the edges, usually a fraction of an inch (a few millimeters). The exact width is in the installation instructions.
- **Skipping acclimatization.** Many manufacturers ask you to leave the boxes in the room for a day or two before installing.
- **Buying the exact amount.** A spare box from the same batch is useful for future repairs, because colors can vary between production runs.
