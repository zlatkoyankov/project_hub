Every tile job loses some tiles. Some are cut and the offcut is too small to use, some break, some have a chip you only notice after opening the box. The question is not whether to buy extra, but how much. "Add 10%" is the rule of thumb everyone repeats, and for many rooms it is fine. For others it is not enough. Here is how to decide.

## Why tile gets wasted

Waste comes from three places:

1. **Cuts at the edges.** Every row that meets a wall needs a cut tile. The more walls, corners and obstacles a room has, the more cuts.
2. **Layout pattern.** Patterns that run at an angle to the walls, such as diagonal and herringbone, create a cut at the end of almost every row, and many of those offcuts cannot be reused.
3. **Breakage and defects.** A few tiles crack during cutting or arrive damaged. Large and thin tiles are more fragile.

## Typical allowances

These are common starting points, not rules. Adjust them for your room and your experience.

| Situation | Extra to buy |
| --- | --- |
| Straight lay, simple rectangular room | about 10% |
| Straight lay with many corners, cabinets or pipes | 10–15% |
| Diagonal layout | about 15% |
| Herringbone or chevron | 15–20% |
| Large-format tiles (60 cm / 24 in and up) | add a few percent more |

If you are new to tiling, lean towards the higher end. A spare box costs less than a second trip, and a second batch may not match.

## Worked example: a 12 × 10 ft kitchen floor

1. **Area:** 12 × 10 = 120 square feet.
2. **Remove what will not be tiled.** A kitchen island that sits on the subfloor, 6 × 3 ft, takes away 18 ft². Tiled area: 102 ft².
3. **Add waste.** The room has a few corners and the tiles go in a straight lay, so 10%: 102 × 1.10 = 112.2 ft².
4. **Convert to boxes.** Suppose the tile is 12 × 24 inches (2 ft² each) and comes 8 to a box, so one box covers 16 ft². 112.2 ÷ 16 = 7.01, which rounds up to **8 boxes**.

Notice how much rounding up matters. The waste allowance gave us 112 ft²; eight boxes give us 128 ft². That leftover is useful, which brings us to the next point.

## Keep a few tiles for later

Tiles are made in batches, and colour and size can vary slightly between them (the batch is often marked on the box as a "lot" or "shade" number). If a tile cracks in five years, the same product may be discontinued or come from a batch that looks different. Keeping a handful of spare tiles from the original purchase makes repairs invisible. When you buy, check that all boxes have the same lot number.

## Metric example: a 2.4 × 1.8 m bathroom

1. Area: 2.4 × 1.8 = 4.32 m².
2. The bath covers 1.7 × 0.75 = 1.28 m², leaving 3.04 m².
3. Diagonal layout, so 15%: 3.04 × 1.15 = 3.50 m².
4. A box covers 1.44 m²: 3.50 ÷ 1.44 = 2.43, so **3 boxes**.

## Don't forget the rest

- **Grout and adhesive** are calculated from area too. Manufacturers give coverage per bag for a given tile size and trowel notch.
- **Skirting or wall tiles** are separate. Measure the perimeter for skirting and wall area for walls, and treat them as their own estimate.
- **Trim pieces** for edges and corners are sold by the piece. Count them, don't estimate them from area.
