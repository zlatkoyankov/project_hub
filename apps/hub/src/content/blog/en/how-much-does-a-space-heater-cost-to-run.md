A plug-in heater is the quickest way to warm a cold room, and also one of the easiest ways to push up an electricity bill without noticing. The good news is that its running cost is simple to work out. You need three numbers: the heater's power, how long it runs and the price you pay for electricity.

## The formula

Electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh is a 1,000-watt appliance running for one hour.

**kWh = watts ÷ 1,000 × hours**

**Cost = kWh × price per kWh**

The power is on the heater's rating label, usually on the back or underneath. In the US most portable heaters are rated at 1,500 W, because that is about the most a standard 120-volt outlet can safely supply. In Europe, 2,000 W models are common.

## Worked example: a 1,500 W heater

The average US residential electricity price was 18.83 cents per kWh in April 2026, according to the US Energy Information Administration. At that price:

| Running time | Energy | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 hour | 1.5 kWh | $0.28 |
| 8 hours a day | 12 kWh | $2.26 a day |
| 8 hours a day for 30 days | 360 kWh | $67.79 a month |

Your own price may be much higher or lower. Rates vary from about 12 cents in some states to over 40 cents in Hawaii, so always use the figure on your bill.

## Thermostats change the picture

The table assumes the heater runs at full power the whole time. Most heaters have a thermostat that switches the element off once the room is warm and back on when it cools. In a well-insulated room the heater may only draw power for part of each hour.

If the heater is on for 8 hours but its element runs about 60% of that time, the monthly cost drops from $67.79 to about $40.67. The [energy cost calculator](/energy-cost-calculator/) has an "average load" field for exactly this.

## Does the type of heater matter?

For plug-in resistance heaters, much less than the adverts suggest. A fan heater, a ceramic heater, a convector and an oil-filled radiator all turn practically all of the electricity they use into heat. A 1,500 W fan heater and a 1,500 W radiator use the same energy per hour at full power.

What differs is how the heat is delivered:

- **Fan and ceramic heaters** warm the air quickly. Good for short bursts in a small room.
- **Oil-filled radiators** take longer to warm up but keep giving off heat after the element switches off. Good for steady heat over several hours.
- **Infrared heaters** warm people and objects in front of them rather than the air. Good for a draughty space where warm air escapes quickly.

The exception is a **heat pump**, which includes most modern air conditioners in heating mode. It moves heat from outside rather than making it, and typically delivers two to four times more heat than the electricity it uses, depending on how cold it is outside. For heating a room for hours every day, it is usually far cheaper than any plug-in heater.

## How to spend less

- **Heat the room you are in, not the house.** This is where a space heater makes sense: a small room for a few hours.
- **Use the thermostat** instead of the highest setting. The room does not warm faster on "max"; the heater just keeps running longer.
- **Close doors and block draughts.** Heat that leaks out has to be replaced.
- **Use a timer** so the heater is not left running in an empty room.
- **Plug it straight into a wall outlet.** Most manufacturers warn against extension cords for heaters, for safety rather than cost.
