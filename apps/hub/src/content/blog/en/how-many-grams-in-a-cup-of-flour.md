**One US cup of all-purpose flour is 120 g.** A 250 ml metric cup of the same flour is about 127 g. Sugar is heavier: 198 g in a US cup and about 209 g in a metric cup. A cup measures volume, and grams depend on how much room the ingredient takes up, so there is no single "grams per cup" figure. Which cup the recipe means matters too: a US cup is 236.6 ml and a metric cup is 250 ml, so the metric one holds about 5.7% more.

## The formula

**grams = millilitres × density (g/ml)**

**millilitres = number of measures × volume of the measure**

The density is how many grams one millilitre of the ingredient weighs. For most ingredients we took a published weight of one US cup or tablespoon and divided it by the volume of that measure: a US cup is exactly 8 US fluid ounces, 236.588 ml, and a US tablespoon is half a fluid ounce, 14.787 ml. For water, milk and sunflower oil we used measured densities, because US tables round liquids to ounces.

## Worked example: 1½ cups of flour

King Arthur Baking gives 120 g for one US cup of all-purpose flour. The recipe asks for 1½ metric cups (250 ml).

1. Density: 120 ÷ 236.588 = **0.5072 g/ml**.
2. Volume: 1.5 × 250 = **375 ml**.
3. Weight: 375 × 0.5072 = **190 g**.

If the same recipe means US cups, the sum is 1.5 × 236.588 = 354.9 ml and 354.9 × 0.5072 = **180 g**. The 10 g difference is enough to change a dough, so find out which cup the recipe uses before you measure.

Where each density comes from:

- **Flour, sugar, powdered sugar, brown sugar, rice, butter, honey, salt, cocoa, starches and oats:** King Arthur Baking, Ingredient Weight Chart. Per US cup: flour 120 g, granulated sugar 198 g, powdered sugar 113 g, brown sugar 213 g, potato starch 152 g, oats 89 g. Rice 99 g per ½ cup, butter 113 g per ½ cup (8 tablespoons), cocoa 42 g per ½ cup, cornstarch 28 g per ¼ cup. Honey 21 g and table salt 18 g per tablespoon.
- **Water:** 0.9982 g/ml at 20 °C (MilliporeSigma).
- **Milk:** 1.03 g/ml, **our own rounding** of 1.0303–1.0313 g/cm³ measured for raw whole milk in a 2021 study.
- **Sunflower oil:** relative density 0.918–0.923 in the Codex Alimentarius standard. We took the middle, 0.9205, which is **our own middle value**, and multiplied it by the density of water.

Ingredients we could not find a source for, such as caster sugar, chopped nuts or grated cheese, are not in the table.

## From grams to cups

A recipe lists 250 g of flour and you only have a cup:

1. Volume: 250 ÷ 0.5072 = **492.9 ml**.
2. In US cups: 492.9 ÷ 236.588 = **2.08**, a little over 2 US cups.
3. In metric cups: 492.9 ÷ 250 = **1.97**, almost 2 metric cups.

Another one: 100 g of granulated sugar is 100 ÷ 0.8369 = 119.5 ml, which is **8.08 US tablespoons**, about 8.

## Why a cup is never exact

- **Flour packs down.** A cup scooped from the bag weighs more than one spooned in and levelled, which is why baking recipes give grams.
- **The ingredient matters.** The table is for fine table salt, solid butter, packed brown sugar and US all-purpose flour. UK plain flour, coarse salt and melted butter will weigh differently.
- **Cups differ.** The US cup is 236.6 ml, the metric cup 250 ml, and some US labels round the cup to 240 ml. The UK Metric Association notes that cups are not a standard UK measure, which is why the grams in a recipe are more reliable.
- **Spoons are level.** We use US spoons, 14.79 ml and 4.93 ml. Metric spoons of 15 ml and 5 ml are within 1.4% of them. Australian recipes use a 20 ml tablespoon, so for those use the millilitres option of the converter.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one weight for every ingredient.** A cup of sugar is not a cup of flour: 198 g against 120 g.
- **Mixing up the cups.** A US cup and a metric cup differ by 13 ml, which is 7 g of flour.
- **Heaped spoons.** The table is for level measures.
- **Trusting a cup for baking.** For bread, cakes and biscuits, weigh on a scale.
