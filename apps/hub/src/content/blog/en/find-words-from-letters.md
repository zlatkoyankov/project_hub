You have seven letters on your rack, or a crossword clue with a few letters filled in, and your mind goes blank. Experienced word-game players are not simply people with bigger vocabularies. They use a handful of techniques that turn a jumble of letters into a short list of candidates. Here are the ones that help most.

## 1. Look for familiar endings first

English words are built from reusable pieces, and the endings are the easiest to spot. Pull them out of your letters first, then see what the rest can make.

- **-ING, -ED, -ER, -EST, -LY** turn short words into longer ones.
- **-S** is the most useful letter in many games because it pluralises almost anything.
- **-TION, -NESS, -MENT, -ABLE** need more letters, but when they fit they produce long words.

With the letters R, A, T, I, N, G and E, setting aside -ING leaves R, A, T, E, which makes RATE, TEAR and TARE. That gives RATING, and with all seven letters, TEARING. The same seven letters also spell GRANITE and INGRATE. A quick check in a word finder confirms which of these your game accepts.

## 2. Then look for beginnings

The same works at the front: **RE-, UN-, IN-, DE-, PRE-, DIS-, OVER-**. If you have R and E, try putting them first: RE + LIST = RELIST, RE + TAIL = RETAIL.

## 3. Pair the letters that like each other

Certain consonants appear together so often that it pays to group them before you start: **TH, CH, SH, PH, WH, CK, QU, NG, ST, TR, BR, PL**. Treat each pair as one block. It reduces the number of pieces you are juggling and quickly shows which arrangements are possible.

Q is almost always followed by U in English, so if you have a Q without a U, look specifically for the short list of exceptions your game allows rather than trying random combinations.

## 4. Check the vowel balance

Most English words alternate consonants and vowels in some rhythm. Seven consonants and no vowels will not make much; neither will five vowels. If your rack is unbalanced, the best move in a game like Scrabble may be a short word that gets rid of the extra letters, or an exchange.

## 5. Anagrams: the same letters, different words

Some letter sets hide several words. These are classic examples:

| Letters | Words |
| --- | --- |
| LISTEN | silent, enlist, tinsel, inlets |
| TRIANGLE | integral, altering, alerting, relating |
| EARTH | heart, hater |
| STONE | notes, tones, onset |

If you find one word that uses all your letters, it is worth checking whether the same letters make others. In Scrabble, a different anagram may fit the board better or reach a premium square.

## 6. Work from the board, not only from your rack

In crosswords and board games you rarely have only your own letters. A letter already on the board can be the start, end or middle of your word. This is where filters help: "starts with T", "ends with S", "contains R". You narrow thousands of candidates to the ones that fit the space.

## 7. Use a word finder to learn, not just to win

A word finder such as [Letterwell](/letterwell/) lists every word you can make from your letters, sorted by length, with optional Scrabble, Words With Friends and Boggle scores. Used during a friendly game, that is cheating. Used afterwards, it is one of the fastest ways to learn: enter the rack you had, see what you missed, and the patterns above start to stick.

A few ways to practise:

- **Random racks.** Generate seven random letters and try to find five words before looking at the answers.
- **Length goals.** Filter by length and hunt specifically for six- and seven-letter words.
- **Use all letters.** Turn on "use all letters" to see whether your rack hides a full-rack anagram.

## A note on dictionaries

Word lists differ. Tournament Scrabble in North America and in the rest of the world use different official lists, Words With Friends has its own, and casual games often allow whatever the players agree on. Letterwell uses an open English word list, so it may include words your game rejects or miss a few it accepts. When it matters, check your game's official dictionary.
