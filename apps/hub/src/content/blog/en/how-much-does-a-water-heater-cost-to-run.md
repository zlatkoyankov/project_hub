For many homes, the water heater is the biggest electricity user after heating and cooling. The good news is that its running cost is easy to estimate, and it hardly depends on the brand. Three things decide it: how much water you heat, by how many degrees, and what you pay per kilowatt-hour.

## How much energy one heating takes

One Btu raises one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit, and a gallon of water weighs about 8.3 pounds. One kilowatt-hour equals 3,412 Btu. That gives the energy for any tank:

**kWh = gallons × 8.3 × temperature rise (°F) ÷ 3,412**

This is physics, not a property of the heater, so it holds for every electric tank. A real heater also loses some heat to the room (more on that below), so treat the result as the minimum.

Example (our own assumptions): a 50-gallon tank, cold water coming in at 55 °F, heated to 120 °F, a rise of 65 °F:

50 × 8.3 × 65 ÷ 3,412 = **7.91 kWh**

With a 3,000 W element, that takes about 2 hours 38 minutes. A more powerful element finishes sooner but uses the same energy. Read the wattage on your tank's label: elements differ between models, and the calculator's preset of 3,000 W is a typical rating, not a rule.

## What it costs per month

The average US residential electricity price was 18.83 cents per kWh in April 2026 (EIA). If the whole tank is heated once a day:

| | Energy | Cost at 18.83¢ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One full heating | 7.91 kWh | $1.49 |
| 30 days | 237 kWh | $44.66 |

Your price is probably different, so use the figure on your own bill. If your utility offers a time-of-use plan with cheaper off-peak hours, running the heater then (with a timer or a smart controller) lowers the cost without changing how much hot water you get.

## Standby losses

A tank loses heat even when nobody draws water, so the element switches on now and then to hold the temperature. The hotter the setting, the bigger the loss. The US Department of Energy says most households only need 120 °F and that a tank set at 140 °F can waste $36 to $61 a year in standby losses. Newer tanks list their standby losses on the label or datasheet; an old tank with poor insulation may lose a meaningful share of your bill.

## How to spend less

- **Set the thermostat to 120 °F** unless you have a specific reason to go higher. Check any health guidance that applies to your household before turning it down much further.
- **Use off-peak power** if your tariff has it.
- **Insulate the first stretch of hot pipe** leaving the tank, especially in a cold basement or garage.
- **Turn the heater to vacation mode or off** when you are away for several days.
- **Flush the tank** as the manufacturer recommends; sediment on the element slows heating.
